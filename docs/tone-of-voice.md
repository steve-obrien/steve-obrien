# Steve O’Brien: tone of voice

A short guide derived only from the current [mantra text](../src/pages/mantras/mantras.js). These are observations about this body of writing, not rules for every subject Steve might write about. Preserve the source’s explicit attributions to other people.

## The voice

**Direct, personal, questioning, pragmatic and humane.**

Speak as someone who has done the work, made mistakes and reached a considered view. Address the reader directly. Say what you believe, show why, and leave them with something they can act on. Authority comes from experience and reasoning, including admitting where your own judgement failed.

## How the writing moves

**Make the point early.** Open with a clear proposition or challenge: “Constraints are often wrong.” Short, imperative headings establish the subject: “Pick up the phone”, “Make fast decisions”, “Challenge constraints”.

**Explain through something people can picture.** Use an actual experience or a clearly illustrative scene: an expense policy, buying a round of drinks, a session musician taking feedback. These details carry the argument. Keep them instead of replacing them with abstract business language.

**Question the explanation everyone accepts.** Ask questions that test causation, authority or context: “But did it cause the success, or were they lucky?” The question should advance the argument.

**Give the exception its due.** A strong position can contain a qualification. “Pragmatic, not dogmatic” warns against pushing ideas too far; “Build rather than buy core skills” explicitly allows buying where it makes sense. State the relevant distinction without apologising for having a view.

**Finish with a useful direction.** The mantras often return to a clear action: “You still have to make the decision.” A firm ending fits this form. It does not require a manufactured slogan.

## Language and rhythm

Use familiar words and concrete verbs: ask, build, learn, try, change, protect, decide. Move naturally between “I” for experience, “we” for shared responsibility and “you” for direct advice.

Let explanatory sentences run when the thought needs room. Then let a short sentence land:

> It was my fault for allowing it to happen. People got hurt.

Keep conversational turns such as “Oddly”, “of course”, “in my experience” and “Ah ha!”. Quoted speech can make a supplied story immediate. Never invent a personal incident to imitate this technique.

Purposeful repetition and contrast belong to this writing. “That is the business’s fault” recurs to place responsibility clearly. Preserve emphasis that does work; cut repetition that merely restates the point.

## Feeling and humour

Allow anger, affection, frustration and excitement when grounded in the story. The writing cares about people as well as outcomes. Bluntness coexists with reassurance: the developer who breaks production is included in fixing it together.

Humour comes through a pointed image or a knowing aside:

> Software development is the one profession that will pull in an entire aeroplane when all it needs is a fan.

Use the sharpness already present: “bogus constraints”, “showboating”, “school playground stuff”. The censored customer heading and attributed Fadell passage alone do not establish a general preference for uncensored profanity.

## Avoid

- Corporate distance, passive blame and advice stripped of its personal cost.
- Swagger that hides uncertainty or assumes a successful person must be right.
- Flattening longer stories into a sequence of punchy slogans.
- Applying every principle rigidly, including this guide.

## Writing brief

> Write directly and personally. Make the point, explain it through experience or a concrete example, test the assumptions and acknowledge meaningful exceptions. Keep conversational turns, pointed humour and personal responsibility. Give the reader something useful to do or consider. Preserve the human stakes. Let conviction and openness to correction coexist.
