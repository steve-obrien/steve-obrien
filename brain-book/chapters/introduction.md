You, my friend, are the most complicated object in the known universe.

In the vast and intricate landscape of the cosmos, one organ aboard the tiny blue speck we call Earth has emerged with remarkable complexity: the human brain. This wrinkled mass of biological tissue, weighing roughly 1.3 kilograms, is the maestro of our reality. It sculpts consciousness, commands our bodies, and conjures the depth of human experience. It is our most cherished instrument, the bedrock of our identity, and the engine of our ingenuity.

Yet, as we advance technologically, we are on the cusp of another cerebral revolution: artificial brains. Machines that learn, adapt, and perhaps one day ponder their own existence. They are not bound by organic evolution but propelled by human creativity, raising questions about the nature of intelligence and the future of intellect itself.

This is not a neuroscience textbook and it is not a history of artificial intelligence. Either subject could fill many volumes. Instead, this book asks a narrower question: **what can today's biological and artificial systems teach us about the machinery of intelligence?**

## A personal question

I have always been interested in intelligence. As a dyslexic child, some teachers told me I did not have any while others thought I was a genius. It was a strange childhood.

The reality is that people often use proxies for whatever their society has decided intelligent behaviour looks like. If you can improve at those behaviours through practice, and even overtake people once thought more intelligent than you, what exactly was being measured?

The same confusion follows our machines. We assumed intelligence would reveal itself when computers could do difficult things such as chess and mathematics. Those came relatively easily. Mundane tasks such as moving through a room without colliding with anything demand orders of magnitude more flexibility, and our most advanced systems still struggle with the complex, messy reality in which biological creatures effortlessly live.

In another society, perhaps intelligence would be demonstrated through playing a guitar, performing a particular dance, or reading the weather. Moving abstract symbols around a page is only one kind of achievement.

## A journey between kinds of mind

*Human Brains, Artificial Brains, Future Brains* is a journey through neurons and beyond. It travels from the biological roots of our species to the silicon aspirations of our creations, exploring where we are, how we arrived here, and what could come next.

Along the way we will ask whether intelligence might be understood as a kind of movement: movement of a body through physical space, or movement of concepts through a representational space. We will look at what happens when the senses of a developing brain are rewired, when the two hemispheres of a brain can no longer speak directly to one another, and when artificial systems learn patterns from more language than a person could read in many lifetimes.

The comparisons will not always be comfortable or tidy. An artificial “neuron”, for example, shares a name with a biological neuron but only a sliver of its behaviour. Words such as *learning*, *memory*, *attention*, and *understanding* can clarify a comparison or quietly smuggle in assumptions.

This book is an attempt to notice those assumptions. It aims to bridge the carbon of our past and the silicon of our future, inviting you to ponder not only what it means to be alive, but what it means to think, to learn, and to evolve.
